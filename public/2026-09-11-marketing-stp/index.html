The short version

Why segment at all (slides 2–3 · 00:04:10-00:14:35)

  • A market is heterogeneous by default — customers differ in what they want, what they can pay, and how they buy. Treating them as one group means the product fits nobody particularly well.
  • Sir used the Nykaa vs local kirana example: same shampoo, completely different buyer, so completely different channel and price point.
  • ⭐ Repeated twice: segmentation is a means, not an end. If two segments respond identically to your marketing mix, they are one segment.

The four bases of segmentation (slides 4–6 · 00:14:35-00:33:20)

  • Geographic — region, city tier, climate. Cheapest to act on, weakest predictor of behaviour on its own.
  • Demographic — age, income, gender, occupation, family stage. Easy to measure, which is why it is overused.
  • ⭐ Behavioural segmentation is the one to reach for first. Demographics are a proxy for behaviour, and a bad one.

Targeting strategies (slides 7–8 · 00:33:20-00:49:05)

  • Undifferentiated — one offer for the whole market. Works only where the product is a commodity and scale is the advantage.
  • Differentiated — separate offers per segment. Higher reach, materially higher cost, and the cost is usually underestimated.
  • ⭐ For a startup the answer is almost always concentrated. You cannot out-spend an incumbent on differentiated marketing.

Positioning and perceptual mapping (slides 9–11 · 00:49:05-01:08:40)

  • Positioning is the place your brand occupies in the customer's mind relative to competitors — not what you say about yourself, but what they believe.
  • A perceptual map plots competitors on two attributes that matter to the buyer. Gaps on the map are candidate positions, but an empty space may be empty because nobody wants it.
  • ⭐ Flagged directly as an exam question: be able to write a positioning statement for a given brand in the prescribed format.

Terms

  • Market Segmentation — Dividing a heterogeneous market into groups that respond differently to the marketing mix.
  • Psychographic Segmentation — Segmenting on lifestyle, values and personality rather than observable demographics.
  • VALS Framework — A psychographic model classifying consumers by primary motivation and resources.
  • Behavioural Segmentation — Segmenting on what customers actually do — usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought, occasion.
  • Concentrated Marketing — Serving one segment properly rather than several partially. The usual choice for a new entrant.
  • Perceptual Mapping — Plotting competing brands against two buyer-relevant attributes to find candidate positions.
  • Points of Parity — Attributes you must have to be considered in a category at all.
  • Points of Difference — Attributes that make a buyer choose you over the alternative.

Why segment at all (slides 2–3 · 00:04:10-00:14:35)

  • A market is heterogeneous by default — customers differ in what they want, what they can pay, and how they buy. Treating them as one group means the product fits nobody particularly well.
  • Sir used the Nykaa vs local kirana example: same shampoo, completely different buyer, so completely different channel and price point.
  • Segmentation is only worth doing if the segments are measurable, substantial, accessible and actionable. He spent a while on 'substantial' — a segment you can describe but can't profitably serve is not a segment.

Stressed: Repeated twice: segmentation is a means, not an end. If two segments respond identically to your marketing mix, they are one segment.

Said in class, not on the deck: Told the story of a startup that segmented by age and found 18-24 and 25-30 behaved identically — wasted six months building two campaigns.

The four bases of segmentation (slides 4–6 · 00:14:35-00:33:20)

  • Geographic — region, city tier, climate. Cheapest to act on, weakest predictor of behaviour on its own.
  • Demographic — age, income, gender, occupation, family stage. Easy to measure, which is why it is overused.
  • Psychographic — lifestyle, values, personality. The VALS framework was covered here. Much harder to measure but explains behaviour the demographic data cannot.
  • Behavioural — usage rate, loyalty status, benefits sought, occasion. Sir called this the most useful one in practice because it segments on what people actually do rather than who they are.

Stressed: Behavioural segmentation is the one to reach for first. Demographics are a proxy for behaviour, and a bad one.

Said in class, not on the deck: Aside on why Indian FMCG still leans heavily on geographic and demographic bases — distribution data is available, psychographic data is not.

Targeting strategies (slides 7–8 · 00:33:20-00:49:05)

  • Undifferentiated — one offer for the whole market. Works only where the product is a commodity and scale is the advantage.
  • Differentiated — separate offers per segment. Higher reach, materially higher cost, and the cost is usually underestimated.
  • Concentrated — one segment, served properly. The default sensible choice for a new entrant with limited capital.
  • Choice depends on company resources, product variability, stage in the product life cycle, and what competitors are already doing.

Stressed: For a startup the answer is almost always concentrated. You cannot out-spend an incumbent on differentiated marketing.

Positioning and perceptual mapping (slides 9–11 · 00:49:05-01:08:40)

  • Positioning is the place your brand occupies in the customer's mind relative to competitors — not what you say about yourself, but what they believe.
  • A perceptual map plots competitors on two attributes that matter to the buyer. Gaps on the map are candidate positions, but an empty space may be empty because nobody wants it.
  • Points of parity are what you must have to be considered at all. Points of difference are why you get chosen over the alternative.
  • The positioning statement format he gave: for [target segment] who [need], our [brand] is the [category] that [key benefit], unlike [main competitor].

Stressed: Flagged directly as an exam question: be able to write a positioning statement for a given brand in the prescribed format.

Said in class, not on the deck: Worked through a positioning statement for Boat headphones on the board — this was not on the slides.
